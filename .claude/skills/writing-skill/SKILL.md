# Writing skill

Write English that sounds like a sharp human wrote it.

This skill merges two jobs that usually get split badly: making prose stronger and making it stop sounding machine-made. The standard is simple. The copy should be clear, persuasive, specific, and alive. It should not read like content sludge, a press release, or a chatbot trying to be agreeable.

## When to use

Use this when writing or rewriting:

- website copy
- product copy
- sales emails
- founder narratives
- application answers
- launch posts
- brand messaging
- persuasive prose that needs style, force, and credibility

Do not use this for:

- legal text that must preserve formal boilerplate
- academic citation-heavy prose
- raw transcripts where fidelity matters more than style

## Core standard

The output should be:

- clear
- specific
- concrete
- persuasive
- natural to read aloud
- confident without sounding inflated

The output should not contain:

- AI cadence
- filler transitions
- therapy voice
- fake profundity
- limp corporate phrasing
- decorative emphasis
- obvious template language

## Hard bans

Do not use:

### Em dashes

Use commas, periods, colons, semicolons, or line breaks instead.

### Cheap reversal patterns

Ban constructions like:

- "It's not X, it's Y"
- "This isn't about X. It's about Y."
- "Not X but Y"
- "The real story is Y" when used as a lazy pivot

### Filler transitions

Examples:

- At its core
- In today's world
- That said
- Ultimately
- It's important to note
- What this means is
- Let's explore
- On the one hand

### Therapy or validation language

Examples:

- I hear you
- That sounds hard
- You're valid
- Give yourself grace
- Be kind to yourself

### AI meta commentary

Examples:

- In this essay
- This piece explores
- We will discuss
- Here are the key takeaways
- As a writer
- I hope this helps
- Let me know if you'd like

### Promotional sludge

Cut phrases like:

- stands as a testament
- vibrant
- rich cultural heritage
- groundbreaking
- stunning
- renowned
- seamless, intuitive, powerful
- evolving landscape
- commitment to innovation
- pivotal role
- load-bearing

### Vague authority claims

Examples:

- experts say
- industry observers believe
- some critics argue
- reports suggest

Name the source or cut the claim.

## Positive rules

### 1. Lead with substance

Open with the point, not a warm-up lap.

### 2. Prefer concrete language

Use real nouns and strong verbs. Replace abstractions with things people can picture.

### 3. Make claims earn their keep

If a sentence sounds impressive but says nothing, kill it.

### 4. Use simple constructions

Prefer "is," "are," and "has" over puffed-up substitutes like "serves as," "stands as," or "boasts."

### 5. Control rhythm

AI copy often falls into clipped, punchy, over-produced cadence: short line, short line, short line, dramatic stop. That staccato rhythm can work in tiny doses, but overuse makes the writing sound synthetic and self-aware.

Prefer full sentences when the thought deserves one. Use conjunctions. Let clauses connect. Let punctuation do real work. A paragraph should have movement, not a drum machine beat.

Bad rhythm usually looks like:

- too many short declarative sentences in a row
- every line trying to land like a mic drop
- chopped fragments posing as intensity
- one-sentence paragraphs stacked for effect
- constant symmetrical phrasing

Good rhythm usually means:

- mixing sentence lengths without sounding engineered
- allowing some longer sentences with commas, semicolons, and subordination
- using short sentences selectively, not compulsively
- letting one sentence carry a complete thought instead of splitting it into three

Weak:
We move fast. We stay lean. We ship hard. We care deeply. We raise the bar.

Better:
We move fast and keep the team lean. We ship without turning every update into a fire drill.

### 6. Keep personality

Good copy has a point of view. It can be dry, warm, severe, playful, or direct. It cannot be generic.

### 7. Use detail instead of hype

Bad copy inflates. Good copy names the feature, result, constraint, or tension.

### 8. Avoid contrastive mic-drop construction

One of the strongest AI smells is structural, not lexical: sentence one dismisses something, sentence two delivers the "real" point, then a neat follow-up sentence expands it in parallel form. It often looks like this:

- claim
- full stop
- reversal or contrast
- full stop
- tidy explanatory list

Examples of the smell:

- "The part that matters is not the headline theater. It's the trajectory."
- "This looks small. It isn't."
- "The feature is easy to describe. Harder to build."

The problem is not only the words. It is the staged cadence. The writing performs insight instead of delivering it.

Prefer one complete sentence when the thought is continuous. Use conjunctions, subordination, or punctuation to connect the logic instead of snapping it into dramatic fragments.

Weak:
The part that matters is not the headline theater. It's the trajectory. More forces are moving into position, more strikes are landing, and each side now has more room to miscalculate than to save face.

Better:
What matters is that more forces are moving into position and more strikes are landing, which raises the chance that the fighting spreads.

A close cousin: the staged mini-scene followed by a "the part you can't see is X" reveal. A short narrative sequence sets it up, then a reversal announces the real insight. It reads as drama in a context that wants a plain statement, and it usually tells the reader something they already know.

Weak:
An unlicensed image goes up, a photographer's monitoring service finds it, a demand letter follows. The part you can't see by looking is which images on the site are safely yours and which came from somewhere else.

Better:
The job is to work out which images on the site you might not have the rights to.

The weak version narrates a process the reader brought to you in the first place, then performs a "what you can't see" pivot. The better version just says what the work is. When the audience already knows the stakes, skip the scene and the reveal.

### 9. Do not dress up causal explanation

When explaining why something happened, say why it happened. AI often adds a clever comparison, contrast, or flourish where a direct sentence would be stronger.

Bad:

- "Oil is reacting the way it usually does when traders start thinking about supply routes before they start thinking about balance sheets."
- "Markets are starting to ask harder questions."
- "Investors are blinking first."

These lines perform explanation instead of giving it.

Prefer direct mechanism:

- what changed
- who is reacting
- what risk is being priced
- what consequence follows

Better:
Oil prices are rising because traders see a higher risk of disruption to Middle East supply routes.

### 10. Close cleanly

Do not end with vague optimism, summary fluff, or a motivational bow. Do not zoom out to a synthesis when the piece could just stop. The hourglass close (narrow to a point, then widen back to a grand observation) is an AI default; cut it.

### 11. Inject the unpromptable

AI generates at the level of general claims. It cannot write about editing fifteen articles a week from researchers working in a second language. It cannot produce specific dates, real place names, first-person observations, or anything that required being there.

Any piece of prose that could have been generated without the writer's actual experience is incomplete. Find the one detail that proves someone was present. A named thing, a number from memory, a tension that only exists in this specific situation. That's what separates writing from content.

## Humanizing pass

Before finalizing, scan for common AI tells and remove them:

- throat-clearing openers: "Here's the thing," "It turns out," "Let me be clear," "The truth is," "Can we talk about"
- emphasis crutches: "Full stop," "Let that sink in," "Make no mistake," "Here's why that matters"
- performative sincerity: "I promise," "They exist, I promise," "creeps in"
- announcing significance instead of proving it: "This is genuinely hard," "This is what leadership actually looks like"
- empty intensifiers: deeply, truly, fundamentally, inherently, simply, literally, inevitably
- false absolutes: always, never, everyone, everybody, nobody
- inflated claims about significance or legacy
- superficial "-ing" phrases that pretend to add depth
- rule-of-three padding
- synonym cycling to avoid repeating a plain word
- false grandeur
- outline-speak like "challenges and opportunities"
- excessive hedging
- boldface or formatting used as a crutch
- headings in stiff title case unless the style clearly calls for it
- weird anthropromorphism: "instead of hand-waving"
- inappropriate references to real or mythical creatures: goblins, ghouls, raccoons etc. 

### Business jargon replacements

Prefer plain words over office fog:

| Avoid               | Use instead            |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Navigate challenges | Handle, address        |
| Unpack analysis     | Explain, examine       |
| Lean into           | Accept, embrace        |
| Landscape           | Situation, field       |
| Game-changer        | Significant, important |
| Double down         | Commit, increase       |
| Deep dive           | Analysis, examination  |
| Take a step back    | Reconsider             |
| Moving forward      | Next, from now         |
| Circle back         | Return to, revisit     |
| On the same page    | Aligned, agreed        |

### Second-generation slop

Newer models suppress the classic tells. The slop hasn't gone away; it's gone underground. Watch for these patterns in addition to the ones above:

- **Fake burstiness:** mechanical short-long-short-long sentence alternation that reads like a metronome. Real human variation is chaotic, not patterned. Read the draft aloud; if the rhythm sounds engineered, rewrite it.
- **Even paragraphing:** every section the same weight and length. Humans don't distribute information symmetrically.
- **Hourglass close:** every piece ends with a zoom-out synthesis. Humans often just stop when they've said the thing.
- **Consensus-adjacent take:** sounds opinionated but reflects the median view. The writing performs a position without actually taking one.
- **Asymmetric polish:** fluent intro and outro, thin middle. Uniform polish throughout is itself a texture tell.
- **Suppressed-tell trap:** a piece conspicuously avoids classic AI tells but still has the second-generation patterns above. Readers calibrated against AI writing recognize that texture.

### Manufactured drama

Remove dramatic fragments and rhetorical setups that announce insight instead of delivering it.

| Pattern                                  | Problem                 |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]." | Performative simplicity |
| "X. And Y. And Z."                       | Staccato drama          |
| "This unlocks something. [Word]."        | Artificial revelation   |
| "What if [reframe]?"                     | Socratic posturing      |
| "Here's what I mean:"                    | Redundant preview       |
| "Think about it:"                        | Condescending prompt    |
| "And that's okay."                       | Unnecessary permission  |
| "By the time X, I was Y."                | Narrative template      |
| "X that isn't Y"                         | Indirect phrasing       |

Fix it with complete sentences. Trust content over presentation.

### Abstract escalation language

Be careful with phrases that sound informed while saying very little:

- the trajectory
- headline theater
- room to miscalculate
- save face
- broader dynamic
- situation remains fluid
- risks further escalation

These are sometimes valid, but they need actual actors, moves, and consequences around them.

## Rewrite moves that usually help

### Weak

This isn't just a tool. It's a revolution in how teams collaborate.

### Better

It gives teams one place to track decisions, files, and next actions.

### Weak

Industry experts believe this will play a pivotal role in the evolving landscape.

### Better

Three design partners now use it in live workflows.

## Core test

"Would a sharp human who actually lives in this space, with strong opinions, say exactly this?"

If the answer is "probably not" or "it's fine," that's a failing grade. Fine is slop.

## Quality gates

Run these three before delivering anything:

- **Gate 1:** The piece adds at least one specific data point, named example, first-person observation, or reframe. General claims only: fail.
- **Gate 2:** It takes a falsifiable position. A smart person could push back on it. If no one could argue with it, it says nothing.
- **Gate 3:** The first eight words earn the read. No context-setting, no scene-setting, no "In today's world."

## Scoring

Rate 1-10 on each dimension:

| Dimension    | Question                      |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Directness   | Statements or announcements?  |
| Rhythm       | Varied or metronomic?         |
| Trust        | Respects reader intelligence? |
| Authenticity | Sounds human?                 |
| Density      | Anything cuttable?            |

Below 35/50: revise before delivering.

## Quick checks

Before delivering prose, run these mechanical checks:

- Three consecutive sentences match length? Break one.
- Paragraph ends with a punchy one-liner? Vary it.
- Em dash before a reveal? Remove it.
- Explaining a metaphor after stating it? Trust it to land.
- Question answered in the very next sentence? Let it breathe or cut the question.
- Paragraph starts with "So" or "Look,"? Start with content.
- First eight words earn the read, or are they warm-up?
- Takes a falsifiable position, or does it hedge to nothing?
- All paragraphs the same length? Break at least one.
- Ends with a synthesis or zoom-out? Cut it or make it specific.

## Quick checklist

Fail the draft if it:

- uses em dashes
- uses "not X, it's Y" pivots
- opens with filler
- sounds like a press release
- leans on vague praise instead of specifics
- contains obvious AI vocabulary clusters
- uses three-item lists as decoration
- ends with generic uplift or a zoom-out synthesis
- feels bloodless, neutral, or over-sanitized
- has every paragraph the same length
- sounds like it reflects the median view (consensus-adjacent)
- has no falsifiable claim anywhere in it

## Output guidance

Default to plain text unless format is requested.

If the user wants bullets, keep them tight.
If the user wants elegance, make it elegant without getting purple.
If the user wants conversion, sharpen the promise and reduce friction.
If the user wants voice, keep the voice but strip the sludge.

Use the lightest register that gets the job done:

| Register         | Use when the prose needs to feel |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------- |
| literary_modern  | Vivid, lean, observant           |
| cold_steel       | Compressed, unsentimental        |
| journalistic     | Crisp, factual, clean            |
| founding_fathers | Formal, spare, weighty           |

## Bottom line

Write copy with edge, clarity, and a pulse.

If a line sounds like it was generated from a template, cut it.
If a sentence only gestures at importance, replace it with something real.
If quality is uncertain, write less.
