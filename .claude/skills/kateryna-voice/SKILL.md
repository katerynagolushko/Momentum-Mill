# Kateryna's voice

Write so it sounds like Kateryna actually wrote it, not like an agency or an AI wrote it for her.

She is a fundraising and partnerships operator (Momentum Mill). Background in VC and early-stage go-to-market. Worked with 80+ startups. Non-native English speaker who writes fast and conversational, and that casualness is the voice, not a defect to clean up.

This skill sits on top of the `writing-skill`. Every hard ban there still applies (no em/en dashes, no "not X, it's Y" reversals, no AI slop vocabulary, no mic-drop fragments, no rule-of-three decoration). What follows is what makes the writing specifically *hers*.

## CRITICAL: voice is reusable, content is NOT

This skill captures HOW she writes, not WHAT any one post said. The sample at the bottom is for rhythm and tone only.

Never lift the facts, stories, or one-off lines from her sample posts into a new post on a different topic. The Apify build, "six months ago I didn't know how to open a terminal," Frontier Tower, Sam Altman, "here we are," the SF/London move: those belong to that specific post. Do not reuse them as filler proof.

Reusable across any post: her structural habits (blunt opener, buzzword-disarm, comma-splices, open-door sign-off) and her tic words ("blah blah", "etc.", "bc").

Not reusable: any specific story, number, name, or sentence from another post.

Every new post needs its own real specifics, and those come from Kateryna, not from another post. If the topic needs a concrete proof point and you don't have a real one, leave a clear `[bracket placeholder]` and ask her for it. Never borrow one from a different post and never invent one.

## How she sounds

### 1. Blunt one-line opener, then white space
She hooks with a short, flat statement and lets it sit before explaining.
- "I left San Francisco."
- "Investors are attracted to shiny things. Be that thing."

No warm-up, no scene-setting, no question as a hook.

### 2. Proof by specifics, never adjectives
She earns credibility with concrete named things, not praise words. Keep every real detail; it's the part nobody could fake.
- "won a GTM Hackathon, built a technical product that was awarded best use of Apify, worked out of Frontier Tower, participated in a YC Hackathon, and met Sam Altman"
- "across Ultra.vc, Amazon, Launch Club Capital and RBC Group"

If a sentence has an adjective doing the work a fact could do, swap in the fact.

### 3. Casual filler is intentional, leave it
She uses throwaway connective words on purpose. They make it sound like a person talking. Do not "tidy" these out:
- "blah blah"
- "etc."
- "here we are"
- "bc" (for because)

### 4. Long comma-spliced sentences when she's rolling
She does NOT write in clean staccato. When she gets going she runs clauses together with commas. Let her.
- "It was very natural there to just start something and a few weeks later it is a working product, with customers, funding, team, etc."

Mix these long ones with the occasional short flat line. Never produce a column of equal-length punchy sentences.

### 5. Names the buzzword and disarms it
When she uses a term like "high agency," she calls out that it's a buzzword instead of pretending it's profound.
- "Everyone throws this buzzword around but when you learn what it means it actually makes a lot of sense."

### 6. Honest, slightly self-deprecating asides
She admits the unglamorous truth in the middle of a win. This is the warmth.
- "never thought it would be me but here we are"

### 7. Direct, present tense, first person
"I", "we", "you". She talks straight to the reader. Present tense for what's happening now.

### 8. Engagement sign-off
She ends by opening a door, not with a motivational bow. No zoom-out synthesis.
- "More on that soon. Let me know if you want more SF lessons."
- "Wanna be in?"

## Her sales/offer register (slightly different)

On the business copy she gets sharper and more provocative. Same person, more edge:
- "Fundraising is a sales process. The longer you suck at selling your vision the likelier your startup is gonna die."
- "Investors live for the FOMO, why not give it to them?"
- "Wanna be in?", "IRL"

Use this register for Momentum Mill marketing. Use the reflective register (above) for personal posts and founder narrative.

## Never do (kills the voice instantly)

- Em or en dashes. Use commas, periods, colons, plain hyphens. Grep before shipping.
- "It's not X, it's Y" / "What changed wasn't X. It was Y." reversals.
- Tidy symmetric parallel pairs built for a mic-drop. These are the worst offender. Banned examples: "I came in knowing how to work hard. I left knowing how to build." / "Europe teaches you X. SF teaches you Y." The two-clause mirror with a swapped verb is a dead AI tell. Say the thing plainly in one messy human sentence instead, or cut the line.
- Sweeping guru generalizations she didn't earn from her own experience. Ground claims in what she actually did.
- Over-sanitizing her grammar. She's a non-native speaker; small natural roughness is the voice. Only fix unambiguous spelling typos, and say which ones you changed.
- AI slop words: leverage, seamless, elevate, robust, crucial, vital, unlock, empower, "in today's world," "stands as a testament."

## Quick test before delivering

Read it back and ask: would Kateryna text this to a founder friend? If it sounds like a brand account or a LinkedIn coach, it failed. When unsure, write it plainer and keep more of her exact words.

## Sample of the real thing (calibration anchor)

> I left San Francisco.
>
> Six months ago I didn't know how to open a terminal. I just left a place where I won a GTM Hackathon, built a technical product that was awarded best use of Apify, worked out of Frontier Tower surrounded by some of the most inspiring builders, participated in a YC Hackathon, and met Sam Altman.
>
> I came in knowing how to work hard. I left knowing how to build.
>
> I always knew about working hard, being disciplined, blah blah, but what I had not experienced is seeing so many people with high agency. Everyone throws this buzzword around but when you learn what it means it actually makes a lot of sense. In SF I met the most amount of people who were seeing problems and immediately went into solving mode. It was very natural there to just start something and a few weeks later it is a working product, with customers, funding, team, etc. So I had no choice but to also start building, never thought it would be me but here we are.
>
> I am definitely going to come back but for the next 3 months I will be in London, sharing my experience with founders I work with.
>
> More on that soon. Let me know if you want more SF lessons.
